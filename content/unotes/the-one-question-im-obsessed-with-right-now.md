There's one question I keep coming back to.

Is it possible to build a truly AI-native company?

Not a company that uses AI.

Not a company that gives everyone ChatGPT, automates a few workflows, and calls itself AI-first.

But a company built from the ground up around the assumption that intelligence is no longer scarce.

A company where AI isn't a tool layered on top of the organisation. It's part of the organisation itself.

I've been thinking about what that actually means.

If you were starting a company today, with no legacy systems, no existing organisational structure, and no assumptions about how work should get done, what would you build?

Would you hire the same people?

Would you create the same departments?

Would you design the same processes?

Would you even define jobs in the same way?

Or would you start with the outcomes you want to achieve, work out what AI can own, and then build the human organisation around what remains?

That's the distinction I'm increasingly interested in: the difference between an AI-enabled company and an AI-native one.

An AI-enabled company takes an existing business model and makes it more efficient.

An AI-native company challenges the business model itself.

It asks whether the fundamental unit of work should still be a person, a role, a department, or something entirely different.

And that leads to some fascinating questions.

What happens when a team of five can do what previously required fifty?

What happens when software doesn't just support decisions but can execute entire workflows?

What happens when the constraint on growth is no longer how quickly you can hire?

And perhaps most importantly, what becomes more valuable when intelligence becomes abundant?

My instinct is that the answer isn't simply smaller teams or lower costs.

It's an entirely different way of designing and operating a company.

One where humans spend more time on judgment, creativity, relationships, and defining what matters, while AI takes on an increasing share of execution.

Of course, there are enormous unanswered questions around reliability, accountability, trust, and how much autonomy we should actually give these systems.

But that's what makes this such an interesting moment.

We're not just figuring out how to use AI in companies. We're figuring out what a company should look like in an AI world.

And I suspect the most interesting businesses of the next decade won't be the ones that adopt AI the fastest.

They'll be the ones that are brave enough to rethink everything from first principles.

That's the question I'm obsessed with right now.

Can you build a truly AI-native company?

And if you can, what would it actually look like?
